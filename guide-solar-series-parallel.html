SOLAR WIRING

Solar: series vs parallel.

The panels still make the same watts. The wiring decides how those watts are presented to the controller.

Series and parallel are not “better” and “worse”. They are two ways of arranging the same panels. Series raises voltage. Parallel raises current. The right choice is the one that stays inside the MPPT's voltage, current and battery-voltage limits in the conditions you actually expect.

Remember: power is roughly conserved. Two 100W panels are still a 200W array whether they are wired as 2S or 2P. What changes is the voltage and current at the array terminals.

Two panels in series

The positive of one panel connects to the negative of the next. The string voltage adds while the current stays close to one panel's current.

In a series string, voltages add. The array current is limited by the panel/string current.

Two panels in parallel

The positives join at a positive connector or busbar, and the negatives join at a negative connector or busbar. The voltage stays close to one panel's voltage while the current adds.

In parallel, voltage stays similar and current adds. Each positive branch should be protected where the array design requires it.

What must be checked before choosing

  1. Cold Voc. Panel open-circuit voltage rises when panels are cold. Add the series Voc values, apply the temperature coefficient, and keep the result below the controller's absolute maximum PV voltage. This is the limit you must not treat casually.
  2. Working voltage. Vmp is the panel voltage at its maximum power point. The controller normally needs the PV voltage to be above the battery voltage by a specified amount before it can start charging. Check the manual rather than assuming “12V panel” means 12V output.
  3. Array current. Parallel strings add current. Check the controller's maximum PV short-circuit current, connector rating, combiner rating and cable ampacity.
  4. Battery voltage. A controller may accept 12V and 24V but not 36V, or may support 48V only on a particular model. Match the actual controller model to the battery bank.
  5. Published PV power. More panel watts can be useful, but a controller may limit the input. That can be intentional oversizing, but it is not the same as having a controller that can deliver every watt on a perfect day.

Shading: the practical warning

Panels in series share a current path. A shaded panel can pull down the current available from that string, especially if the shade covers a meaningful part of the panel. Parallel wiring can make the unaffected string continue producing, but it is not a magic cure: bypass diodes, shade position and the controller's operating range still matter.

Keep panels in a string matched as closely as possible. Do not casually mix different panel types, orientations or heavily different shade patterns on one MPPT input.

Never design from the label alone. Check the panel's cold-temperature Voc, the controller's absolute maximum PV voltage, its PV short-circuit limit, its battery-voltage support and its published PV-power guidance. The calculator on this site is a planning aid, not permission to exceed a manual.

Try the numbers

For two 425W panels with a 37.5V Voc, putting them in series gives about 75V STC Voc and 850W of array power. The cold-corrected value is the one to use for the controller voltage check. A 12V battery then needs a controller whose 12V PV recommendation, voltage limit, current limit and battery support all make sense together.
