SOLAR FROM FIRST PRINCIPLES

Solar: series vs parallel.

How to read a panel label, what an MC4 connector does, and how panel wiring changes the voltage and current seen by the controller.

A solar panel has a positive lead and a negative lead. With more than one panel, those leads can be connected in series, in parallel, or in a mixture of both. The choice changes voltage and current, so it must be made from the panel label and the controller manual.

The one-line rule: series adds voltage; parallel adds current. Total rated watts are the sum of the panel watts either way.

MPPT means Maximum Power Point Tracking. An MPPT solar charge controller sits between the panels and battery. It continually adjusts the panel operating point to collect useful power, then converts that power into the voltage and current the battery needs.

First, read the panel label

A typical label contains several numbers that look similar but answer different questions. These ratings are normally measured at STC, which means Standard Test Conditions: laboratory light of 1,000W per square metre, a solar-cell temperature of 25°C and a standardised light spectrum. STC lets panels be compared fairly; it is not a forecast of what the roof will produce every day.

Pmax

The headline panel wattage at STC. It is calculated near the maximum-power point: Vmp × Imp.

Voc

Voltage with the panel disconnected from a load. Voc rises as the panel gets colder, so cold-corrected Voc is used for the controller's absolute voltage check.

Vmp

The panel's working voltage when it is producing maximum power under the stated test conditions.

Isc

Short-circuit current. It is a design and test value used to check the controller, connectors, cable and protection.

Imp

Current at maximum power. This is closer to the panel's useful operating current in strong sun.

Temperature coefficient

How much a value changes with temperature. A negative Voc coefficient means voltage rises when the panel is colder than the 25°C STC cell temperature.

What is an MC4 connector?

MC4 is the common locking solar connector found on panel leads. One panel normally has a positive lead and a negative lead with complementary connector housings. The connectors are designed for outdoor PV wiring and latch together so they cannot be pulled apart accidentally.

  • Use PV cable, contacts and housings that are specified to work together, with the correct crimping tool.
  • Check the moulded + and − markings. Do not decide polarity from the apparent “male” or “female” shape alone.
  • Do not mix different connector makes simply because both are advertised as “MC4-compatible”. A mechanically connectable pair is not necessarily an approved matched pair.
  • Do not disconnect MC4 connectors while current is flowing. Isolate the circuit in the order required by the equipment manuals.
  • Use proper release tools. Do not force the locking tabs with pliers or leave a connector where water can sit around a damaged seal.

Two panels in series

Series makes one continuous chain. Panel 1 positive connects to Panel 2 negative. The two unused outer leads become the array output to the MPPT solar charge controller.

The conductors land on Panel 1 − and Panel 2 + at the outer ends, with one MC4 series link between Panel 1 + and Panel 2 −.

Series: voltage adds; current stays about the sameVmp: 20V + 20V = 40V. Imp: about 5A. Power: 40V × 5A = 200W.

Two panels in parallel

Parallel keeps both positives together and both negatives together. A positive MC4 Y-branch combines the positive leads; a matching negative Y-branch combines the negative leads. The two combined outputs go to the controller.

Both positive conductors meet only at the positive Y-branch. Both negative conductors meet only at the negative Y-branch. Neither wire floats or changes polarity.

Parallel: current adds; voltage stays about the sameVmp: about 20V. Imp: 5A + 5A = 10A. Power: 20V × 10A = 200W.

The six checks that decide the layout

  1. Battery voltage support. Confirm that the exact controller model supports your 12V, 24V, 36V or 48V battery bank.
  2. Cold array Voc. Add the Voc of panels in series, then correct it for the coldest expected panel temperature. It must stay below the controller's absolute maximum PV voltage.
  3. Working array Vmp. The array must provide enough voltage above the battery for the controller to start and continue charging. The required margin is in the controller manual.
  4. Array Isc. Add the short-circuit current of parallel strings and compare it with the controller's published PV short-circuit limit.
  5. Published PV watts. Compare total panel watts with the manufacturer's recommendation for your battery voltage. Oversizing can mean controlled clipping on strong days; it does not make an over-voltage or over-current design safe.
  6. Cable, connectors and protection. Parallel wiring raises current. Every connector, branch, combiner, isolator and cable must be rated and protected for the design.

Why cold weather changes the voltage check

Solar-panel voltage rises as the cells get colder. The Voc printed on the label is normally an STC value at a 25°C cell temperature. A clear winter morning can therefore produce a higher open-circuit voltage than the label value.

The panel datasheet gives a Voc temperature coefficient, usually as a negative percentage per degree Celsius. The calculator uses that figure and your expected minimum panel temperature to estimate cold Voc. This is why the controller check uses cold-corrected Voc rather than only the number printed next to Voc.

What happens in shade?

Panels in series share one current path, so a shaded panel can reduce the current from that string. Most modern panels include bypass diodes that can let current bypass shaded cell groups, but their behaviour depends on where the shade falls.

Parallel branches are more independent: an unshaded branch can continue contributing even if another branch is weak. That does not make parallel automatically best. The higher current may need larger cable and different protection, and the lower array voltage still has to remain high enough for the MPPT to operate.

A worked 425W example

Take two matching panels, each rated at 425W, 37.5V Voc, 31.5V Vmp, 14.2A Isc and 13.5A Imp.

Two in series850W · 75V Voc · 63V Vmp · 14.2A Isc
Two in parallel850W · 37.5V Voc · 31.5V Vmp · 28.4A Isc

Those are STC figures. The series option still needs its 75V Voc corrected upwards for cold weather. The parallel option needs equipment and cable able to accept the combined current. Both layouts need an MPPT that supports the battery voltage and total PV power.

Try your panels in the calculator

Do not connect by trial and error. A controller can reduce excess power by clipping, but it cannot make excessive PV voltage safe. Follow the current panel and controller manuals, and isolate before changing connectors.
