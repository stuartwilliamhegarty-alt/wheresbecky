STARTING FROM ZERO

Planning a 12V system.

What every part does, what the letters mean, and the order to make decisions before buying cable or batteries.

A campervan electrical system is easier to understand when you stop seeing it as a cupboard full of equipment. It is one complete circuit: energy is collected, stored, distributed to an appliance, and returned to the battery.

If you remember one rule: work out what you need to power before choosing the battery, chargers, cable or fuses.

Electricity in four ideas

Voltage (V)

Voltage is the electrical push. A system called “12V” does not sit at exactly 12.0V; battery voltage changes while it charges and discharges.

Current (A)

Current is the amount of electricity flowing now. It is measured in amps. More current means more heating in the cable, which is why high-current equipment needs thicker cable.

Power (W)

Power is how quickly a device is using energy. It is measured in watts. The basic relationship is watts = volts × amps.

Energy (Wh)

Energy is power used over time. A 40W fridge consuming power for five hours has used 200 watt-hours: 40W × 5h = 200Wh.

Why watts and amps are not interchangeable

A 120W appliance on 12V draws about 10A because 120 ÷ 12 = 10. The same 120W on 24V draws about 5A. The appliance power is still 120W, but the higher-voltage system carries it with less current.

DC, AC and polarity

DC means direct current. Batteries, solar panels, USB sockets, many fridges and most campervan lighting use DC. It has a positive (+) and negative (−) side, and polarity must be correct.

AC means alternating current. UK household sockets supply AC. A van uses an inverter to turn battery DC into mains-style AC. That conversion wastes some energy, so a 500W AC appliance takes more than 500W from the battery side.

PV means photovoltaic—the solar-panel side of the system. MPPT means Maximum Power Point Tracking, the method used by a solar charge controller to obtain useful power from the panels and turn it into controlled battery charging.

What each part does

Leisure battery

Stores energy for the living area. It is separate from the starter battery in a typical installation.

Busbar

A solid common connection point. A positive busbar distributes the supply; a negative busbar gathers the return cables.

Fuse

Protects the cable from overheating if too much current flows. The fuse rating must suit the cable and the expected load. A fuse is not an on/off switch.

Isolator

A switch intended to disconnect part of the system for maintenance or an emergency. It does not replace a fuse.

Shunt

A calibrated measuring point in the battery-negative cable. A battery monitor uses the tiny voltage drop across it to calculate current and estimate battery state of charge.

MPPT solar charger

Maximum Power Point Tracking. It finds the panel's useful operating point and converts that power into the correct charging voltage and current for the battery.

BMS

Battery Management System. In a lithium battery it monitors cells and can disconnect charging or loads if voltage, current or temperature moves outside its limits.

Inverter

Turns battery DC into mains-style AC. Large inverters draw very high current from a 12V battery and need short, heavy cables and suitable protection.

The abbreviations you will meet

SOC

State of charge: the battery monitor's estimate of how full the battery is, shown as a percentage.

Ah

Amp-hours: battery charge capacity. It is useful only when the battery voltage is also known.

Wh

Watt-hours: stored or used energy. Wh makes it easier to compare loads and batteries operating at different voltages.

PV

Photovoltaic: the technical word for equipment that turns light into electricity. “PV array” simply means the group of solar panels.

Voc

Open-circuit voltage: panel voltage when nothing is drawing power. It rises in cold conditions and is used to check the controller's absolute voltage limit.

Vmp

Voltage at maximum power: the panel voltage near its most productive working point under the stated test conditions.

Isc

Short-circuit current: a panel test value used when checking controller, cable and protection limits. It is not the normal charging current.

Imp

Current at maximum power: the panel current at its rated maximum-power point.

STC

Standard Test Conditions: a laboratory reference used for panel ratings—1,000W/m² of light, a 25°C cell temperature and a standard solar spectrum. It allows fair comparison; it is not a promise of everyday output.

DoD

Depth of discharge: the percentage of battery capacity that has been used. An 80% DoD means 20% remains.

Connectors and cable ends

A sound circuit depends as much on its joints as its cable. Loose or badly crimped connections add resistance, create heat and cause voltage drop.

  • MC4: the common locking, weather-resistant connector fitted to solar-panel leads. Panels usually arrive with one positive and one negative MC4-style lead. Use the correct crimp tool and matching contacts; the moulded connector shape alone is not a substitute for checking the + and − markings.
  • MC4 Y-branch: a matched pair of splitters used to join panel strings in parallel. Parallel wiring increases current, so the cable, connector and controller limits must be checked.
  • Ring lug: a metal eye crimped onto larger cable and bolted to batteries, busbars, fuses and shunts. Use the correct lug for both the cable size and stud size.
  • Ferrule: a sleeve crimped over fine-stranded cable before it enters a compatible screw or clamp terminal. Use one only where the equipment maker permits it.
  • Heat-shrink: protects and supports a finished crimp, but it cannot rescue a poor mechanical joint.
Connector caution: “MC4-compatible” does not guarantee that connectors from different manufacturers form a safe matched pair. Keep mated connectors from the same product family, use the specified contacts and tooling, and do not unplug a PV connector while it is carrying current.

Plan the system in this order

  1. List every load. Record watts or amps, quantity, hours per day, and whether it runs on 12V DC or through an inverter.
  2. Calculate daily energy. Multiply watts by hours for each item, then add the watt-hours together.
  3. Choose reserve time. Decide how long the system should last without useful solar or driving.
  4. Choose the battery. Check usable capacity, continuous current, surge current, charging limits, temperature limits and the battery-management-system rating.
  5. Choose charging sources. Solar, alternator and mains chargers must all support the battery chemistry and voltage.
  6. Draw the circuit. Put every fuse, isolator, busbar, shunt, connector and cable termination on the drawing.
  7. Size cables and protection. Use current, cable length, acceptable voltage drop, installation method and temperature—not guesswork.
  8. Check access. Fuses and isolators must remain reachable after the furniture is built.

A simple energy example

Fridge45W × 8h = 360Wh
Four lights4 × 3W × 5h = 60Wh
Laptop65W × 3h = 195Wh
Daily total615Wh

At a nominal 12V, 615Wh is roughly 51Ah before allowing for conversion losses, reserve and the battery's usable-capacity limit. This is why daily watt-hours are the useful starting point.

Before switching on

  1. Check every cable against the drawing and label both ends.
  2. With the system unpowered, check continuity and confirm that positive is not shorted to negative.
  3. Check every crimp with a firm pull and tighten terminals to the manufacturer's stated torque.
  4. Fit fuses only after the cable and polarity checks are complete.
  5. Follow each equipment manual's power-up order. Many solar controllers should see the battery before the panels.
  6. Test one circuit at a time, watching voltage, current and unexpected heat.
This is a planning guide, not a wiring certificate. Batteries can supply enough fault current to start a fire. Follow the manuals, use correctly rated DC equipment, and have unfamiliar high-current work checked by a competent person.
