ELECTRICAL PLANNING

Planning a 12V system.

A sensible order for working out what the van needs, how big the battery should be, and where the fuses belong.

A 12V system is not one big purchase. It is a chain of decisions: what the loads use, how long they need to run, where that energy comes from, and how every cable is protected when something goes wrong.

The short version: list the loads first, size the battery from real use, choose charging sources, then size cables and protection. Do not start with the biggest battery you can afford.

The system in one picture

This is the usual layout for a small campervan. Positive current leaves the battery through a main fuse and isolator, reaches a positive busbar, then goes through an individual fuse to each load. The negative side returns through a shunt so the battery monitor can measure what goes in and out.

A simplified wiring layout. The exact fuse sizes and cable sizes depend on the equipment, cable length, installation method and local requirements.

Start with the loads

Write down each appliance, its current or wattage, and how long it runs in a day. A fridge may be rated at 45W but does not necessarily draw 45W continuously; use a realistic daily estimate. A laptop, inverter or heater can be a different story because their high-power periods matter.

Energy used (Wh) = power (W) × time (hours)Example: a 40W device running for 5 hours uses 200Wh.

Once the daily watt-hours are known, divide by the battery voltage to estimate amp-hours. Allow for usable-capacity limits and losses; a battery should not normally be planned around its entire nameplate capacity.

The useful electrical words

Voltage — V

Electrical pressure. A 12V battery is nominally 12V, but its actual voltage moves with state of charge, load and charging. Voltage tells you the system level; it does not tell you how much energy is stored.

Current — A

Amps are the flow of electricity at a moment in time. Cable heating and fuse selection are mainly current questions.

Power — W

Watts are the rate of doing work. The relationship is W = V × A. At 12V, a 120W load draws about 10A before conversion losses.

Capacity — Ah

Amp-hours describe charge capacity. A 100Ah battery can theoretically deliver 100A for one hour, or 10A for ten hours, but real usable capacity depends on chemistry, temperature, discharge rate and cut-off settings.

SOC

State of charge. The estimated percentage of energy remaining in the battery. SOC is not the same thing as voltage, especially while a load or charger is active.

Voc and Vmp

Voc is a solar panel's open-circuit voltage. Vmp is its voltage at maximum power. Use cold-corrected Voc when checking the controller's maximum PV voltage; Vmp is the useful working point under load.

Isc and Imp

Isc is short-circuit current and Imp is current at maximum power. Array current rises when panels are paralleled. Check both the controller's PV-current limit and the cable design.

MPPT

Maximum Power Point Tracking. The controller continuously looks for the voltage and current combination that extracts the most useful power from the panels, then converts it to the battery's charging voltage.

Choose the battery after the load estimate

  1. Total the daily Wh. Include the inverter's losses for AC appliances.
  2. Choose autonomy. Decide how many days you want to cover without meaningful charging.
  3. Allow usable capacity. LiFePO₄, AGM and other chemistries have different sensible limits.
  4. Check the battery's continuous and peak current limits. A large battery does not automatically make an inverter safe.

Charging and protection

Every charging source needs to suit the battery chemistry and its voltage limits. Solar, alternator chargers and mains chargers normally meet at the battery side of the system, but each source still needs its own correctly sized protection and cable run.

Put overcurrent protection as close as practical to the source of available fault current. The main battery fuse is there because a battery can deliver enormous current into a short circuit. Individual branch fuses protect the cable to each load. An isolator is for safely disconnecting equipment; it is not a substitute for a fuse.

Take care: this is a planning guide, not a wiring certificate. Follow the battery, charger and inverter manuals, use correctly rated DC components, and have unfamiliar high-current work checked by a competent person.

A sensible commissioning order

  1. Label both ends of every cable before it disappears into the build.
  2. Check polarity and continuity with the system unpowered.
  3. Fit fuses only after the cable and termination checks are complete.
  4. Power up the battery side first, then connect charging sources as their manuals specify.
  5. Test each circuit separately and record voltages, current and any unexpected heat.
